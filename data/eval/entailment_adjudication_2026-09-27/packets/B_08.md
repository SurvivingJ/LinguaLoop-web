**Passage:**

The concept of quantum entanglement is fundamental to the advancement of quantum computing architectures. Entanglement describes a peculiar correlation between quantum particles, such as qubits, where their fates become intrinsically linked, regardless of the physical distance separating them. When two or more qubits are entangled, measuring the state of one instantaneously influences the state of the others. This non-local connection is a resource that quantum computers can harness to perform computations that are intractable for classical machines.

In the realm of quantum computing, qubits serve as the basic units of information. Unlike classical bits, which can only represent a 0 or a 1, qubits can exist in a superposition of both states simultaneously. This property, combined with entanglement, allows quantum computers to explore a vast number of possibilities concurrently. When qubits are entangled, their combined state possesses more information than the sum of their individual states. This is where the power of quantum algorithms truly shines.

Quantum algorithms are designed to exploit these quantum phenomena to solve specific problems more efficiently. For instance, Shor's algorithm, which can factor large numbers exponentially faster than classical algorithms, relies heavily on entanglement to achieve its speedup. Similarly, Grover's algorithm for searching unsorted databases also leverages entanglement to gain a quadratic advantage. The architecture of a quantum computer must be carefully designed to create, maintain, and manipulate these entangled states with high fidelity.

The physical implementation of qubits and the mechanisms for inducing and controlling entanglement are diverse and actively researched. Superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and photonic systems are some of the leading candidates for building robust quantum computers. Each approach presents its own set of challenges in terms of scalability, error correction, and maintaining the delicate quantum states. The ability to generate large-scale, highly entangled states reliably is a critical bottleneck in scaling up quantum computing architectures.

Furthermore, the theoretical understanding of how best to utilize entanglement for different computational tasks is continuously evolving. Researchers are exploring new entanglement-based protocols for quantum communication, quantum sensing, and advanced quantum simulations. The interplay between entanglement, quantum error correction codes, and the design of quantum algorithms is a complex but vital area of study. As our ability to control and engineer entangled systems improves, so too will the power and applicability of quantum computing. The quest to build fault-tolerant quantum computers hinges significantly on mastering the nuances of quantum entanglement.
